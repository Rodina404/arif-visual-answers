Chain-of-thought prompting is a way of prompting a large language model in which, before you ask your actual question, you give the model a few solved examples, and each of those examples contains not only the question and the answer but also the reasoning steps that a person would go through to get from the question to the answer. For instance, the paper uses an example about Roger, who has 5 tennis balls and buys 2 more cans with 3 balls each, and the example explains that Roger started with 5 balls, that 2 cans of 3 balls make 6, that 5 plus 6 is 11, and only then says that the answer is 11, which teaches the model to imitate the same pattern and write out its own steps when it gets a new question. Standard prompting, on the other hand, gives the model examples that contain only the question and the final answer without any explanation, so the model learns to jump straight to an answer, and on problems that need several steps this often makes it wrong, as in the same figure where the model is asked about a cafeteria that had 23 apples, used 20 and bought 6 more, and standard prompting answers 27, which is incorrect, while with a chain of thought the model writes that 23 minus 20 is 3 and 3 plus 6 is 9 and arrives at the correct answer of 9. The paper explains that this approach has several attractive properties: it lets the model break multi-step problems into intermediate steps so more computation can go to harder problems, it gives an interpretable window into how the model reached its answer so you can see where the reasoning went wrong, it can be used for many kinds of tasks such as arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning, and it is easy to use because you do not need to train or finetune the model but only need to include examples with chains of thought in the prompt, although it is important to remember that the benefit only appears in sufficiently large models, since smaller models tend to produce illogical chains of thought and can even perform worse.
